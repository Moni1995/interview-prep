1. Foundations

Experiment, sample space, events, axioms

  • Experiment — a procedure with an uncertain result (toss a coin, roll two dice, will it rain tomorrow).
  • Outcome — one result of a single trial.
  • Sample space \( \Omega \) (or \(S\)) — the set of all outcomes. Die: \( \Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\} \). Two dice: 36 ordered pairs.
  • Event — any subset of \( \Omega \). "Even number" \( = \{2,4,6\} \).
  • Probability — a function \(P\) assigning a number to each event, obeying the three axioms.
Kolmogorov axioms \[ 0 \le P(A) \le 1, \qquad P(\Omega)=1, \qquad P\Big(\bigcup_i A_i\Big)=\sum_i P(A_i)\ \text{ for pairwise disjoint } A_i . \] Consequences: \(P(\varnothing)=0\); \(P(A^c)=1-P(A)\); if \(A\subseteq B\) then \(P(A)\le P(B)\).

For equally likely outcomes (classical definition): \[ P(A)=\frac{\#\text{ favourable outcomes}}{\#\text{ total outcomes}} . \]

Example — sum of two dice Question. Roll two fair six-sided dice. What is the probability that the two numbers add up to 8?
Step 1 — Write down the sample space. Treat the dice as distinguishable (say, a red die and a blue die). Then an outcome is an ordered pair like (3, 5), and there are \(6\times6=36\) of them, each with probability \(1/36\). Keeping the pairs ordered is what makes them equally likely — that is why we don't merge (3, 5) and (5, 3) into one outcome.
Step 2 — Count the favourable outcomes. List the pairs that sum to 8: (2, 6), (3, 5), (4, 4), (5, 3), (6, 2) — five pairs. Doing this for every sum gives:

Step 3 — Divide. \(P(\text{sum}=8)=\dfrac{5}{36}\approx 0.139\). And by the complement rule, \(P(\text{sum}\ne 8)=1-\dfrac5{36}=\dfrac{31}{36}\).
Trap A number like \(-0.00001\) or \(1.001\) can never be a probability (axiom 1). A probability of exactly 0 or exactly 1 is allowed.
Apply it — quality control A batch holds 20 items, 4 of them defective. An inspector draws 2 at random. What is the probability of catching at least one defective?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Choose the sample space (classical rule). All \(\binom{20}{2}=190\) pairs are equally likely.
2 — Go through the complement ("at least one" trap): the complement is "both fine": \(\binom{16}{2}=120\) pairs.
3 — Apply \(P(A)=1-P(A^c)\): \(P=1-\dfrac{120}{190}=\dfrac{70}{190}=\dfrac{7}{19}\approx0.368\).
Sanity check: drawing 2 of 20 with 20 % defective — a bit more than a third catching one feels right; with replacement it would be \(1-0.8^2=0.36\), very close.

Inclusion–exclusion \[ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B) \] \[ P(A\cup B\cup C)=P(A)+P(B)+P(C)-P(A\cap B)-P(A\cap C)-P(B\cap C)+P(A\cap B\cap C) \] For disjoint events the intersection terms vanish: \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)\).
Example — can two events be disjoint? Question. Two events have \(P(A)=0.6\) and \(P(B)=0.7\). Could \(A\) and \(B\) be disjoint (i.e. never happen together)?
Step 1 — Recall what disjoint would imply. Disjoint means \(P(A\cap B)=0\), so inclusion–exclusion would give \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-0=0.6+0.7=1.3\).
Step 2 — Spot the contradiction. No probability can exceed 1, so \(P(A\cup B)=1.3\) is impossible. Therefore \(A\) and \(B\) cannot be disjoint — they must overlap.
Step 3 — How big must the overlap be? Rearranging \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\le 1\) gives \(P(A\cap B)\ge P(A)+P(B)-1=0.3\). So the overlap is at least 0.3 (and at most \(\min(0.6,0.7)=0.6\), since the intersection can't be bigger than either event).
Takeaway. Whenever \(P(A)+P(B)>1\), the events are forced to overlap. This exact question (with 0.6 and 0.7) is Question 1 of the mock exam.
Example — decomposing a union into disjoint pieces Question. Is the identity \(P(A\cup B\cup C)=P(C)+P(A\cap C^c)+P(B\cap A^c\cap C^c)\) true for any three events? (This is Question 4 of the mock exam, and it looks scarier than it is.)
Step 1 — Read each term in words. \(P(C)\) = "in \(C\)". \(P(A\cap C^c)\) = "in \(A\) but not in \(C\)". \(P(B\cap A^c\cap C^c)\) = "in \(B\) but in neither \(A\) nor \(C\)".
Step 2 — Check the three pieces cover everything. Take any outcome in \(A\cup B\cup C\). If it's in \(C\), the first term catches it. If not in \(C\) but in \(A\), the second term catches it. If it's in neither \(C\) nor \(A\), it must be in \(B\), and the third term catches it. Every point of the union is caught.
Step 3 — Check no piece is counted twice. The second term explicitly excludes \(C\); the third excludes both \(A\) and \(C\). The three pieces are disjoint by construction, so their probabilities simply add (axiom 3). The identity is true.
Takeaway. Slicing a union into non-overlapping pieces ("take \(C\) whole, then what's new in \(A\), then what's new in \(B\)") is the standard way to avoid double counting. Draw the Venn diagram once and check each region is covered exactly once — that's the whole proof.

De Morgan: \((A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c\) and \((A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c\). Useful for "at least one" questions: \(P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{none})\).

Apply it — tool-usage survey In a team, 60 % use Python, 40 % use R, and 25 % use both. Find: (a) P(uses Python or R), (b) P(uses neither), (c) P(uses exactly one).
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Inclusion–exclusion: \(P(Py\cup R)=0.6+0.4-0.25=0.75\).
2 — Complement: \(P(\text{neither})=1-0.75=0.25\).
3 — Disjoint decomposition: exactly one \(=P(Py\cup R)-P(\text{both})=0.75-0.25=0.50\) (or \((0.6-0.25)+(0.4-0.25)\)).
Bonus: are Python and R use independent? \(P(Py)P(R)=0.24\ne0.25\) — almost, but not quite.

2. Counting

Core formulas
  • Multiplication rule: \(k\) independent choices with \(n_1,\dots,n_k\) options give \(n_1 n_2\cdots n_k\) outcomes.
  • Permutations (order matters): \(\;{}^nP_r=\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\)
  • Combinations (order irrelevant): \(\;\dbinom{n}{r}={}^nC_r=\dfrac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\)
  • With repetition allowed, \(r\) picks from \(n\) symbols: \(n^r\).
Example — all six rolls distinct Question. Roll a fair die 6 times. What is the probability that all six outcomes are different (i.e. each face 1–6 appears exactly once)?
Step 1 — Count all possible sequences. Each roll has 6 options and rolls are independent, so by the multiplication rule there are \(6\times6\times\dots\times6=6^6=46\,656\) equally likely sequences.
Step 2 — Count the favourable sequences. "All different" over 6 rolls of a 6-sided die means the sequence is some ordering of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. The first roll can be any of 6 faces, the second any of the 5 remaining, and so on: \(6\times5\times4\times3\times2\times1=6!=720\).
Step 3 — Divide. \[ P=\frac{6!}{6^6}=\frac{720}{46\,656}\approx 0.0154 \approx 1.5\,\%. \] Takeaway. Denominator = "how many total sequences" (repetition allowed → \(n^r\)); numerator = "how many sequences satisfy the condition" (no repetition → falling product / factorial). Keeping those two counting modes straight solves most counting questions.
Example — the birthday problem Question. In a room of \(n\) people, what is the probability that at least two share a birthday? (Assume 365 equally likely birthdays, no leap year.)
Step 1 — Flip to the complement. "At least one shared birthday" is messy (a pair, two pairs, a triple…). Its complement — "all \(n\) birthdays are different" — is one clean event. Compute that, then subtract from 1.
Step 2 — Build "all different" person by person. Person 1 can have any birthday: \(\frac{365}{365}\). Person 2 must avoid 1 day: \(\frac{364}{365}\). Person 3 must avoid 2 days: \(\frac{363}{365}\). Continuing, \[ P(\text{all different})=\frac{365}{365}\cdot\frac{364}{365}\cdot\frac{363}{365}\cdots\frac{365-n+1}{365}. \] Step 3 — Plug in numbers. For \(n=23\): \(P(\text{all different})\approx0.493\), so \(P(\text{some shared})\approx0.507\) — better than a coin flip with only 23 people! For \(n=30\): shared \(\approx0.706\); for \(n=50\): \(\approx0.97\).
Why so high? Intuition compares 23 people to 365 days, but what matters is the number of pairs: \(\binom{23}{2}=253\) chances for a match. Memorise the anchors: 23 → 50 %, 30 → 70 %, 50 → 97 %.
Example — same class (Jack & Jill) Question. 60 students are randomly split into 3 classes of 20 each. Jack and Jill are two of the students. What is the probability they end up in the same class?
Step 1 — Fix Jack first. The split is random, so put Jack wherever he lands — it doesn't matter which class. His class now has 19 remaining seats.
Step 2 — Ask where Jill goes. From Jill's point of view there are 59 remaining seats (60 minus Jack's), all equally likely, and 19 of them are in Jack's class: \[ P(\text{same class})=\frac{19}{59}\approx 0.322 . \] Why isn't it \(1/3\)? \(1/3\) would be right if Jill's class were chosen independently of Jack's. But the classes have fixed sizes: Jack occupying a seat leaves his class with only 19 of the 59 remaining seats, slightly fewer than a third. This is sampling without replacement — finite groups make events dependent. (Mock-exam Question 10.)
Example — subset of a hand (Anita & Babita) Question. Anita picks 4 cards from a 52-card deck, notes them, and puts them back. Babita then draws 8 cards from the same full deck. What is the probability that all four of Anita's cards are among Babita's 8? (Mock-exam Question 7 — the wording trips people up, so restate it: 4 specific cards are "marked"; someone draws 8; what's the chance all 4 marked cards are in the draw?)
Step 1 — Count all possible draws. Babita's hand is an unordered set of 8 cards from 52: \(\binom{52}{8}\) equally likely hands.
Step 2 — Count the favourable hands. A favourable hand must contain the 4 marked cards (only \(\binom44=1\) way to include them) plus 4 more cards chosen from the other 48: \(\binom{48}{4}\) ways.
Step 3 — Divide. \[ P=\frac{\binom{48}{4}}{\binom{52}{8}}=\frac{194\,580}{752\,538\,150}\approx 2.6\times10^{-4}. \] Takeaway. When a hand must contain specific cards, build the numerator in two parts: (forced cards) × (free choices for the rest). The trap answer \(\binom{48}{4}/\binom{52}{4}\) mixes up whose draw defines the sample space.
Example — symmetry beats counting Question. Cards are dealt one at a time from a shuffled 52-card deck. What is the probability that the 13th card dealt is a King?
The slick argument. A shuffle makes every ordering of the deck equally likely, so position 13 is just as likely to hold any particular card as position 1 is. The question "is the 13th card a King?" therefore has the same answer as "is the first card a King?": \[ P=\frac{4}{52}=\frac{1}{13}\approx 0.077 . \] Why you're allowed to do this. Nothing was revealed about cards 1–12 — if you had seen them, the probability would change. Unconditionally, though, "13th card" is not special in any way; by symmetry all 52 positions are interchangeable. Whenever a question asks about "the \(k\)-th card/draw" with no other information, the answer equals the first-draw probability. (Mock-exam Question 8.)
Example — ordered comparison (4-sided die) Question. Roll a fair 4-sided die twice. What is the probability the first roll is strictly higher than the second? (Mock-exam Question 5.)
Step 1 — Sample space. Ordered pairs (first, second): \(4\times4=16\) equally likely outcomes.
Step 2 — Use symmetry instead of listing. Split the 16 pairs into three groups: ties, first > second, first < second. There are 4 ties: (1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (4,4). The remaining \(16-4=12\) pairs split evenly between "first higher" and "second higher" — swapping the two rolls turns each pair of one kind into the other, so neither can be more likely.
Step 3 — Conclude. \[ P(\text{first}>\text{second})=\frac{12/2}{16}=\frac{6}{16}=\frac38 . \] (Check by listing: (2,1),(3,1),(3,2),(4,1),(4,2),(4,3) — exactly 6.)
General shortcut. For an \(n\)-sided die: \(P(\text{tie})=\frac1n\) and \(P(\text{first}>\text{second})=\frac{1-1/n}{2}\).
Example — geometric probability (coin on a grid) Question. A coin of diameter 1 inch is tossed onto a table ruled with a square grid of lines 2 inches apart. What is the probability the coin lands entirely inside a square, touching no line? (Mock-exam Question 23.)
Step 1 — Reduce the coin to its centre. The coin avoids a line exactly when its centre is at least one radius (0.5 in) away from that line. So track only the centre point.
Step 2 — Find the "safe" region. Within each 2×2 cell, the centre must stay ≥ 0.5 from all four edges — that leaves a smaller square of side \(2-2\times0.5=1\), i.e. a 1×1 safe square centred in the cell.
Step 3 — Probability = area ratio. The centre is uniformly distributed over the cell, so \[ P=\frac{\text{safe area}}{\text{cell area}}=\frac{1\times1}{2\times2}=\frac14 . \] Takeaway. With continuous outcomes, counting becomes measuring: probability = favourable area (or length, or volume) ÷ total area. The one modelling move to remember is "replace the object by its centre and shrink the region by the radius".
Apply it — a five-card hand From a standard 52-card deck you are dealt 5 cards. Probability of exactly 2 aces?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Count total hands (combinations, order irrelevant): \(\binom{52}{5}=2\,598\,960\).
2 — Build the favourable hands with the multiplication rule: choose 2 of the 4 aces, \(\binom42=6\); choose the other 3 cards from the 48 non-aces, \(\binom{48}{3}=17\,296\).
3 — Divide: \(P=\dfrac{6\times17\,296}{2\,598\,960}=\dfrac{103\,776}{2\,598\,960}\approx0.0399\).
Rule check: the numerator must classify every card of the hand (2 aces AND 3 non-aces) — forgetting the \(\binom{48}{3}\) factor is the classic error.

3. Conditional probability

Definition \[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B)>0 . \] "Probability of A given B": restrict the sample space to \(B\) and renormalise.
Example — a card, given it's a face card Question. One card is drawn from a standard deck. You're told it is a face card (Jack, Queen, or King). What is the probability it is a King?
Step 1 — Restrict the sample space. "Given it's a face card" throws away all 40 non-face cards. The new universe is the 12 face cards (J, Q, K in each of 4 suits), still equally likely.
Step 2 — Count inside the new universe. 4 of those 12 are Kings: \[ P(\text{King}\mid\text{face card})=\frac{4}{12}=\frac13 . \] Note the answer changed from \(4/52\) to \(4/12\) — conditioning shrinks the denominator to the event you were told about.

How to read it. "Given follows rules" means: look only at the first column (2 050 drivers) and ask what fraction had an accident: \[ P(\text{Accident}\mid\text{Follows})=\frac{50}{2050}\approx 0.024 \qquad\text{vs}\qquad P(\text{Accident}\mid\text{Doesn't})=\frac{500}{5500}\approx 0.091 . \] Takeaway. Conditioning on an event = restricting attention to that row or column and renormalising by its total. Contrast with the joint probability \(P(\text{Accident}\cap\text{Follows})=50/7550\approx0.007\), which divides by the whole table — a different question.
Example — two tests Question. In a class, 25 % of students passed both tests and 45 % passed the first test. What percentage of those who passed the first test also passed the second? (Mock-exam Question 34.)
Step 1 — Translate to symbols. "Passed both" is the intersection: \(P(T_1\cap T_2)=0.25\). "Passed the first" is \(P(T_1)=0.45\). The question — "of those who passed the first, how many passed the second" — is exactly \(P(T_2\mid T_1)\).
Step 2 — Apply the definition. \[ P(T_2\mid T_1)=\frac{P(T_1\cap T_2)}{P(T_1)}=\frac{0.25}{0.45}\approx 0.556\approx 56\,\% . \] Sanity check. The answer must be larger than 25 % (we shrank the denominator from 100 % of students to the 45 % who passed test 1) — and it can't exceed 100 %. The phrase "of those who…" in a word problem is your cue that a conditional probability is being asked for.
Example — the two-child problem Question. A family has two children. You learn that at least one of them is a girl. What is the probability that both are girls? (Mock-exam Question 2 — most people's instinct says \(1/2\); the answer is \(1/3\).)
Step 1 — Write the full sample space in birth order (older, younger), each combination equally likely at \(1/4\): GG, GB, BG, BB.
Step 2 — Apply the information. "At least one girl" rules out only BB. Three equally likely cases remain: GG, GB, BG.
Step 3 — Count. Exactly one of those three is "both girls": \[ P(\text{GG}\mid\text{at least one girl})=\frac{1}{3}. \] Why intuition says 1/2. It imagines pointing at a specific child ("the older one is a girl"). That's different information: it removes BB and BG, leaving GG vs GB — and then the answer really is \(1/2\). The lesson: what you compute depends on exactly which event you conditioned on, so always write the sample space before and after the information arrives.
Apply it — spam filtering by hand 30 % of incoming email is spam. The word "free" appears in 40 % of spam. Overall, 16 % of all email contains "free". An email contains "free" — probability it is spam?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Get the joint with the chain rule: \(P(\text{spam}\cap\text{free})=P(\text{spam})\,P(\text{free}\mid\text{spam})=0.3\times0.4=0.12\).
2 — Apply the conditional-probability definition: \(P(\text{spam}\mid\text{free})=\dfrac{P(\text{spam}\cap\text{free})}{P(\text{free})}=\dfrac{0.12}{0.16}=0.75\).
3 — Read the direction: we flipped from \(P(\text{free}\mid\text{spam})=0.4\) to \(P(\text{spam}\mid\text{free})=0.75\) — they are different questions with different answers.

Chain (multiplication) rule

\[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B\mid A)=P(B)\,P(A\mid B) \] \[ P(A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3)=P(A_1)\,P(A_2\mid A_1)\,P(A_3\mid A_1\cap A_2) \] In particular \(P(A\cap B\cap C^c)=P(A)\,P(C^c\mid A)\,P(B\mid A\cap C^c)\) — an identity that holds for any events (with positive-probability conditions).
Example — drawing without replacement Question. A bag holds 3 black and 3 white marbles. You draw two marbles, one after the other, without putting the first back. What is the probability both are black?
Step 1 — First draw. 3 of the 6 marbles are black: \(P(B_1)=\frac36=\frac12\).
Step 2 — Second draw, updated bag. Given the first was black, the bag now holds 2 black and 3 white, so \(P(B_2\mid B_1)=\frac25\). This updating step is where "without replacement" enters.
Step 3 — Chain rule. \[ P(B_1\cap B_2)=P(B_1)\,P(B_2\mid B_1)=\frac36\cdot\frac25=\frac15=0.2 . \] Compare. With replacement the draws would be independent and the answer would be \(\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14\). Without replacement is smaller — the first black draw makes a second one harder. Always ask which regime the problem is in before multiplying.
Apply it — urn, three draws An urn holds 5 red and 3 blue marbles. Draw 3 without replacement. Find (a) P(red, red, blue in that order), (b) P(all three the same colour).
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Chain rule, updating the urn each draw: \(P(RRB)=\dfrac58\cdot\dfrac47\cdot\dfrac36=\dfrac{60}{336}=\dfrac{5}{28}\approx0.179\).
2 — For (b), split into disjoint cases: \(P(RRR)=\dfrac{5\cdot4\cdot3}{8\cdot7\cdot6}=\dfrac{60}{336}\), \(P(BBB)=\dfrac{3\cdot2\cdot1}{336}=\dfrac{6}{336}\).
3 — Add (disjoint events): \(\dfrac{66}{336}=\dfrac{11}{56}\approx0.196\).
Rule check: each factor conditions on everything drawn so far — that is \(P(A_1)P(A_2\mid A_1)P(A_3\mid A_1\cap A_2)\) in action.

Independence

\(A\) and \(B\) are independent iff \[ P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B) \iff P(A\mid B)=P(A). \] Independence is not the same as disjointness: disjoint events with positive probability are maximally dependent (if one happens the other cannot).
Worked examples — multiplying (and when you may)
  • Marble and coin. Draw a marble from a jar with 4 red and 3 black, and separately flip a fair coin. P(red marble and heads)? The two experiments don't influence each other → independent → multiply: \(\frac47\times\frac12=\frac27\).
  • Two dice rolls. Roll a die twice. P(first roll is a 2 and second roll is not a 4)? Different rolls of a die are independent, so \(P=\frac16\times\frac56=\frac5{36}\). (Mock-exam Question 3 — note "not 4" is \(\frac56\), the complement of one face.)
  • Same colour twice, with replacement. Jar with 3 red and 1 white marble; draw one, put it back, draw again. P(both draws are the same colour)? Split into two disjoint cases and use independence inside each: both red \((\frac34)^2=\frac9{16}\); both white \((\frac14)^2=\frac1{16}\). Add: \(\frac{10}{16}=\frac58\). (Mock-exam Question 20 — "with replacement" is what allowed squaring.)
  • Checking dependence with numbers. In a cafeteria, 60 % of customers order a bagel, 50 % order pizza. You're told \(P(\text{bagel}\mid\text{pizza})=0.7\). Is ordering a bagel independent of ordering pizza? Compare: independence would require \(P(\text{bagel}\mid\text{pizza})=P(\text{bagel})=0.6\). Since \(0.7\ne0.6\), they are dependent — knowing someone ordered pizza raises the chance they ordered a bagel. The test is always "does conditioning change the probability?"
Example — when is an event independent of itself? Question. Can event \(A\) be independent of \(A\)? (Mock-exam Question 14 — it sounds like a trick, but it's a definition check.)
Step 1 — Write the definition with \(B=A\). Independence of \(A\) and \(A\) requires \(P(A\cap A)=P(A)\,P(A)\).
Step 2 — Simplify the left side. \(A\cap A=A\), so the condition is \(P(A)=P(A)^2\).
Step 3 — Solve. \(p=p^2\) means \(p(1-p)=0\), so \(p=0\) or \(p=1\).
Interpretation. An event carrying real uncertainty (0 < p < 1) always "informs on itself": knowing \(A\) happened makes \(A\) certain. Only the trivial events — impossible or certain — carry no information to give.
Trap — gambler's fallacy After three tails, a fair coin is still 50/50 on the next toss. Independent trials have no memory. Past losses do not make heads "due".
Apply it — system reliability Two independent servers are up 95 % and 90 % of the time. Find (a) P(both up) — a "series" system, (b) P(at least one up) — a "parallel" system.
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Independence product rule: \(P(\text{both})=0.95\times0.90=0.855\).
2 — Parallel via the complement: \(P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{both down})=1-0.05\times0.10=0.995\).
3 — Where independence enters: both steps multiplied probabilities — legal only because the servers fail independently. If they share a power supply, \(P(\text{both down})>0.005\) and the parallel system is worse than computed. Always ask "is independence justified?" before multiplying.

Law of total probability

If \(B_1,\dots,B_n\) partition \(\Omega\) (disjoint, exhaustive): \[ P(A)=\sum_i P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i) . \] Two-case version: \(P(A)=P(A\mid B)P(B)+P(A\mid B^c)P(B^c)\).
Example — mixed coins, expected tosses Question. There are two coins. Coin A shows heads with probability \(\frac12\); coin B with probability \(\frac13\). You pick coin A with probability \(\frac14\) (so coin B with probability \(\frac34\)), then toss that same coin repeatedly until the first head. How many tosses do you expect to need? (Mock-exam Question 11.)
Step 1 — Recognise the two stages. First something random happens (which coin), then the experiment runs with that coin. Whenever a problem has this shape, condition on the first stage.
Step 2 — Solve each branch separately. "Tosses until the first head" with heads-probability \(p\) is a Geometric(\(p\)) variable, whose mean is \(1/p\) (on average you need \(1/p\) tries for something that succeeds a fraction \(p\) of the time). So: with coin A, \(E[T\mid A]=1/\tfrac12=2\) tosses; with coin B, \(E[T\mid B]=1/\tfrac13=3\) tosses.
Step 3 — Weight the branches by their probabilities (law of total expectation): \[ E[T]=P(A)\,E[T\mid A]+P(B)\,E[T\mid B]=\tfrac14\times 2+\tfrac34\times 3=0.5+2.25=2.75 . \] Sanity check. The answer must land between the two branch answers (2 and 3), closer to 3 because coin B is more likely — 2.75 fits.
Apply it — traffic split between two models 70 % of requests go to model A (error rate 2 %), 30 % to model B (error rate 6 %). (a) Overall error rate? (b) An error occurred — probability it came from A?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Law of total probability over the partition {A, B}: \(P(E)=0.7\times0.02+0.3\times0.06=0.014+0.018=0.032\).
2 — For (b), definition of conditional probability: \(P(A\mid E)=\dfrac{0.014}{0.032}=0.4375\).
3 — Interpret: A handles 70 % of traffic but causes only ~44 % of the errors; B causes 56 % from just 30 % of traffic. This two-step (total probability, then divide) is Bayes' theorem — next section.

4. Bayes' theorem

\[ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}=\frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B\mid A)P(A)+P(B\mid A^c)P(A^c)} \]
\(P(A)\)prior — belief before seeing the evidence
\(P(B\mid A)\)likelihood — how probable the evidence is if \(A\) is true
\(P(B)\)evidence / marginal — total probability of the evidence (from the law of total probability)
\(P(A\mid B)\)posterior — updated belief
Bayes "reverses" a conditional: it turns \(P(\text{evidence}\mid\text{cause})\) into \(P(\text{cause}\mid\text{evidence})\).

Recipe that never fails: (1) write down the prior of each hypothesis; (2) write the likelihood of the observed evidence under each hypothesis; (3) multiply; (4) divide by the sum. Or think in natural frequencies: imagine 100 000 people and count.

Medical-test examples

Example — ELISA / cancer screening Prior (prevalence) \(=0.00148\); sensitivity (true-positive rate) \(=0.93\); specificity (true-negative rate) \(=0.99\) ⇒ false-positive rate \(=0.01\). \[ P(D\mid +)=\frac{0.93\times0.00148}{0.93\times0.00148+0.01\times0.99852}=\frac{0.001376}{0.001376+0.009985}\approx0.121 . \] Only ~12 % of positives are truly sick, because the disease is rare and false positives from the huge healthy group swamp the true positives.
Frequency view: of 100 000 people, 148 are sick → 138 test positive; 99 852 are healthy → 999 test positive. 138 / (138 + 999) ≈ 12 %.
Example — typhoid test Question. Rob tests positive for typhoid. The test detects typhoid in 80 % of people who have it (sensitivity), but also comes back positive in 10 % of people who don't (false-positive rate). 1 % of the population has typhoid. What is the probability Rob actually has it? (Mock-exam Question 38.)
Step 1 — Name the pieces. Prior \(P(T)=0.01\); likelihoods \(P(+\mid T)=0.8\) and \(P(+\mid T^c)=0.1\).
Step 2 — Total probability of a positive. Positives come from two disjoint sources — sick people testing positive and healthy people testing positive: \(P(+)=0.8\times0.01+0.1\times0.99=0.008+0.099=0.107\).
Step 3 — Bayes. \[ P(T\mid+)=\frac{0.8\times0.01}{0.107}=\frac{0.008}{0.107}\approx 0.075\approx 7.5\,\% . \] Frequency check (per 1 000 people). 10 have typhoid → 8 test positive. 990 don't → 99 test positive. Of the \(8+99=107\) positives, only 8 are real: \(8/107\approx7.5\,\%\). The false positives outnumber the true ones 12-to-1 because the disease is rare.
Example — second-interview call Question. After a first interview, 50 % of candidates get a call for a second interview. Among those who got the call, 95 % had felt good about their first interview; among those who didn't get a call, 75 % had also felt good. You felt good about your interview — what is the chance you'll get the call? (Mock-exam Question 22.)
Step 1 — Spot the direction flip. You're given \(P(\text{felt good}\mid\text{call})\) but asked for \(P(\text{call}\mid\text{felt good})\) — that reversal is the signature of a Bayes problem.
Step 2 — Total probability of "felt good". \(P(\text{good})=0.95\times0.5+0.75\times0.5=0.475+0.375=0.85\).
Step 3 — Bayes. \[ P(\text{call}\mid\text{good})=\frac{0.95\times0.5}{0.85}=\frac{0.475}{0.85}\approx0.56\approx 56\,\% . \] Interpretation. Feeling good only nudged your chances from 50 % to 56 % — because 75 % of the rejected candidates felt good too. Evidence that is common under both hypotheses discriminates weakly.
Example — identical twins Question. 30 % of twin births are identical twins, 70 % fraternal. Identical twins are always the same sex (half both-boy, half both-girl). Fraternal twins are like two independent children: ¼ both boys, ¼ both girls, ½ mixed. You have twins and are told both are girls — what is the probability they are identical? (Mock-exam Question 37.)
Step 1 — Priors and likelihoods. \(P(I)=0.3\), \(P(F)=0.7\). Evidence is "both girls" (GG): \(P(GG\mid I)=0.5\), \(P(GG\mid F)=0.25\).
Step 2 — Total probability of the evidence. \(P(GG)=0.3\times0.5+0.7\times0.25=0.15+0.175=0.325\).
Step 3 — Bayes. \[ P(I\mid GG)=\frac{0.15}{0.325}\approx0.46 . \] Interpretation. The prior said 30 % identical, but "both girls" is twice as likely for identical twins (0.5 vs 0.25), so the belief rises to 46 % — not past 50 %, because the prior favoured fraternal. Posterior = prior reweighted by how well each hypothesis explains the evidence.
Example — two-tailed coin Question. Jack has two coins: one normal, one faulty with tails on both sides. He picks one at random (50/50) and tosses it: tails. What is the probability he tossed the faulty coin? (Mock-exam Question 39.)
Step 1 — Likelihood of "tails" under each hypothesis. Faulty coin: tails is certain, \(P(T\mid\text{faulty})=1\). Real coin: \(P(T\mid\text{real})=\frac12\).
Step 2 — Total probability of tails. \(P(T)=1\times\frac12+\frac12\times\frac12=\frac34\).
Step 3 — Bayes. \[ P(\text{faulty}\mid T)=\frac{1\times\frac12}{\frac34}=\frac{2}{3} . \] Equivalent counting view. The toss showed one of the coins' tail faces. There are 3 tail faces in play (2 on the faulty coin, 1 on the real one), all equally likely to be showing — and 2 of the 3 belong to the faulty coin. Hence \(2/3\), not the intuitive \(1/2\): the faulty coin has more ways to produce what you saw.
Apply it — fraud flagging 0.5 % of transactions are fraudulent. A model flags 95 % of fraud but also 3 % of legitimate transactions. A transaction is flagged — probability it is fraud?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Name the pieces: prior \(P(F)=0.005\); likelihoods \(P(\text{flag}\mid F)=0.95\), \(P(\text{flag}\mid F^c)=0.03\).
2 — Evidence via total probability: \(P(\text{flag})=0.95\times0.005+0.03\times0.995=0.00475+0.02985=0.0346\).
3 — Bayes: \(P(F\mid\text{flag})=\dfrac{0.00475}{0.0346}\approx0.137\).
4 — Frequency check (per 100 000): 500 fraud → 475 flagged; 99 500 legit → 2 985 flagged; \(475/3460=13.7\,\%\). Most flags are false alarms because fraud is rare — the base rate dominates.

Sequential (Bayesian) updating

The core idea: today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior. After the first positive ELISA (previous section) the belief moved from 0.148 % to ≈ 12 %. Now the same person takes a second, independent ELISA and it is also positive. Redo Bayes, but start from 12 % instead of 0.148 %: \[ P(D\mid++)=\frac{0.93\times0.12}{0.93\times0.12+0.01\times0.88}=\frac{0.1116}{0.1116+0.0088}\approx0.93 . \] Two weak-ish positives compound into near-certainty. (Mock-exam Question 30.)

The odds shortcut (worth learning once). Instead of probabilities, track odds \(=\dfrac{P}{1-P}\). Bayes then becomes a single multiplication: \[ \text{posterior odds}=\text{prior odds}\times LR, \qquad LR=\frac{P(\text{evidence}\mid D)}{P(\text{evidence}\mid D^c)} . \] Here each positive test has \(LR=\dfrac{0.93}{0.01}=93\) ("a positive is 93× more likely if you're sick"). Prior odds \(=\dfrac{0.00148}{0.99852}\approx\dfrac1{675}\). After two independent positives: odds \(\approx\dfrac{93\times93}{675}\approx12.8\), and converting back, \(P=\dfrac{12.8}{13.8}\approx0.93\) — same answer, no denominators to carry. A third positive would push it to ≈ 0.999.
Apply it — a second, independent flag The same transaction is also flagged by a second, independent model with the same rates. Now what is the probability of fraud?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Odds form: after flag 1 the odds are \(\dfrac{0.137}{0.863}\approx0.159\).
2 — Likelihood ratio of a flag: \(LR=\dfrac{0.95}{0.03}\approx31.7\).
3 — Multiply: posterior odds \(=0.159\times31.7\approx5.04\) ⇒ \(P=\dfrac{5.04}{6.04}\approx0.83\).
Rule check: yesterday's posterior became today's prior; independence of the two models (given the truth) is what justified multiplying by \(LR\) once more. Two weak signals compound into a strong one.

Monty Hall and friends

Monty Hall The game. Three doors: one hides a car, two hide goats. You pick Door 1. The host — who knows where the car is and always opens a goat door you didn't pick — opens Door 3, showing a goat. He offers you a switch to Door 2. Should you take it? (Mock-exam Question 15.)
The quick argument. When you picked Door 1 it had a \(1/3\) chance of the car; the other two doors together held \(2/3\). The host's reveal is guaranteed to happen no matter where the car is (he can always find a goat among the other doors), so it tells you nothing new about your door — Door 1 stays at \(1/3\). But the whole \(2/3\) that belonged to "Doors 2 or 3" now sits entirely on the still-closed Door 2. Switch: you win \(2/3\) of the time.
The Bayes check (if the quick argument feels like a trick). Compare how likely "host opens Door 3" is under each location of the car: car behind 1 → he picks randomly between 2 and 3, probability \(\tfrac12\); car behind 2 → he is forced to open 3, probability \(1\); car behind 3 → impossible, \(0\). Posterior for Door 2: \[ P(\text{car@2}\mid\text{opens 3})=\frac{1\cdot\frac13}{\frac12\cdot\frac13+1\cdot\frac13+0\cdot\frac13}=\frac{2}{3} . \] Still not convinced? Play it with 100 doors: you pick one, the host opens 98 goat doors. Your door was right \(1/100\) of the time — the one door he carefully left closed carries the other \(99/100\). Run the simulator below and watch the frequencies.
Challenge from the article — who really knew the answer? Question. On a multiple-choice question, 60 % of students genuinely know the answer. Even knowing it, they mis-click 15 % of the time (so they answer correctly 85 % of the time). Students who don't know guess and are right 25 % of the time. A student answered correctly — what is the probability they actually knew it?
Step 1 — Set up the two hypotheses. Knew (\(K\), prior 0.6) vs guessed (\(K^c\), prior 0.4). Evidence: "answered correctly", with \(P(C\mid K)=0.85\) and \(P(C\mid K^c)=0.25\).
Step 2 — Total probability of a correct answer. \(P(C)=0.85\times0.6+0.25\times0.4=0.51+0.10=0.61\).
Step 3 — Bayes. \[ P(K\mid C)=\frac{0.51}{0.61}\approx0.836 . \] Interpretation. Of every 100 students, 51 answer correctly because they know and 10 by lucky guessing — so a correct answer means "knew it" about 84 % of the time. This is exactly how you should think about, say, a passed screening question: some passes are real skill, some are noise, and the split depends on both the base rate and the guessing rate.
Apply it — Monty Hall with an ignorant host Same game, but this time the host does NOT know where the car is: he opens one of the other two doors at random, and it happens to reveal a goat. Should you still switch?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Write the likelihood of the observation under each hypothesis (you picked door 1, host opened door 3, goat visible):
car@1: host opens 3 with prob \(\tfrac12\), always goat → \(\tfrac12\). car@2: opens 3 with prob \(\tfrac12\), it is a goat → \(\tfrac12\). car@3: goat cannot be shown → 0.
2 — Bayes with equal priors: \(P(\text{car@1}\mid\text{obs})=\dfrac{1/2}{1/2+1/2}=\dfrac12\) — switching now gains nothing.
3 — Compare with the classic host (who always shows a goat deliberately): likelihoods were \(\tfrac12,1,0\) → switch wins \(\tfrac23\).
Moral: the information content of evidence depends on the process that produced it, not just on what you saw. This is the single most transferable Bayes lesson.

5. Random variables

A random variable \(X\) maps each outcome to a number, so we can do arithmetic with uncertainty. Coin: \(X=1\) for heads, \(0\) for tails. Two dice: \(Y=\) sum \(\in\{2,\dots,12\}\), not equally likely.

  • Discrete — countable values (die faces, number of defects, clicks). Described by a PMF.
  • Continuous — any value in an interval (rainfall, height, lifetime). Described by a PDF; probability of any single exact value is 0.

PMF, PDF, CDF

PMF (discrete): \(p(x)=P(X=x)\), with \(\sum_x p(x)=1\).
PDF (continuous): \(f(x)\ge0\), \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1\), and \[ P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx . \] Formally \(f(x)=\lim_{\Delta\to0}\dfrac{P(x\le X\le x+\Delta)}{\Delta}\) — a density, not a probability; \(f(x)\) can exceed 1.
CDF (both): \(F(x)=P(X\le x)\), non-decreasing from 0 to 1; \(P(a<X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)\); for continuous \(X\), \(f=F'\).
Trap "A fly lives between 4 and 6 days. Probability it dies at exactly 5 days?" For a continuous lifetime, \(P(X=5)=\int_5^5 f=0\). Only intervals have positive probability (e.g. \(P(4.9\le X\le5.1)\)).
Apply it — an unnormalised density A random variable has density \(f(x)=c\,x^2\) on \([0,2]\) (0 elsewhere). Find \(c\), \(P(X>1)\), and the median.
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Normalisation axiom: \(\int_0^2 c\,x^2dx=c\cdot\tfrac{8}{3}=1\Rightarrow c=\tfrac38\).
2 — Build the CDF: \(F(x)=\int_0^x \tfrac38t^2dt=\dfrac{x^3}{8}\) on \([0,2]\).
3 — Use it: \(P(X>1)=1-F(1)=1-\tfrac18=\tfrac78\).
4 — Median solves \(F(m)=\tfrac12\): \(m^3=4\Rightarrow m=4^{1/3}\approx1.587\).
Rule check: the density rises like \(x^2\), so most mass sits near 2 — a median of 1.59 (well above the midpoint 1) is consistent.

Expectation and variance

\[ E[X]=\sum_x x\,p(x)\quad\text{or}\quad\int x f(x)\,dx, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]=E[X^2]-\mu^2,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)} \] Linearity (always, even if dependent): \(E[aX+bY]=aE[X]+bE[Y]\).
For independent \(X,Y\): \(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)\); in general \(\mathrm{Var}(aX)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)\).
Example — insurance policy Question. An insurer sells a $240 000 one-year life-insurance policy to a 25-year-old for a premium of $210. The probability she survives the year is 0.999592. What is the insurer's expected profit on this policy? (Mock-exam Question 12.)
Step 1 — List the outcomes from the insurer's point of view. She survives (probability 0.999592): the insurer keeps the $210 premium and pays nothing. She dies (probability \(1-0.999592=0.000408\)): the insurer keeps $210 but pays out $240 000, netting \(210-240\,000=-239\,790\).
Step 2 — Weight each outcome by its probability. \[ E[\text{profit}]=0.999592\times210+0.000408\times(-239\,790)\approx 209.91-97.83\approx\$112 . \] (Cleaner arithmetic: the premium is received in both cases, so \(E=210-240\,000\times0.000408=210-97.92\approx\$112\).)
Takeaway. Fix whose perspective you're computing (insurer, not customer — for the customer it's \(-\$112\)), enumerate every outcome with its cash amount, and remember money paid out enters with a minus sign.
Example — lottery ticket Question. A lottery ticket costs $5. You pick two digits (each 0–9, repeats allowed) and one letter (A–Z); the draw picks its own two digits and letter. If your whole ticket matches (both digits in order + letter) you win $10 405. If your letter matches but the digits don't fully match, you win $100. Otherwise nothing. Ahmed plays "04R" — what is his expected net profit? (Mock-exam Question 27.)
Step 1 — Probability of each prize. Grand prize: all three symbols match, \(P=\frac1{10}\times\frac1{10}\times\frac1{26}=\frac1{2600}\). Letter-only prize: letter matches (\(\frac1{26}\)) and the two digits are not both right (\(1-\frac1{100}=\frac{99}{100}\)): \(P=\frac1{26}\times\frac{99}{100}=\frac{99}{2600}\).
Step 2 — Expected winnings (before the ticket cost). \[ E[\text{winnings}]=10\,405\times\frac1{2600}+100\times\frac{99}{2600}=4.00+3.81=\$7.81 . \] Step 3 — Subtract the cost. \(E[\text{net}]=7.81-5=+\$2.81\).
Takeaways. (1) Define the prize events so they are disjoint — that's why the letter-only prize carries the factor \(\frac{99}{100}\), keeping the grand-prize case out of it. (2) Don't forget to subtract the cost at the end. (3) Yes, this expectation is positive — a deliberately unusual lottery; always check the sign rather than assuming lotteries lose money.
Example — expected wins at roulette Question. A roulette wheel has 38 slots: 18 red, 18 black, 2 green. You bet on red 5 times. How many wins do you expect? (Mock-exam Question 17.)
Step 1 — One game. \(P(\text{red})=\frac{18}{38}\approx0.4737\).
Step 2 — Linearity of expectation. The number of wins is the sum of 5 win/lose indicators, and the expectation of a sum is the sum of expectations — no binomial table, no independence argument needed: \[ E[\text{wins}]=5\times\frac{18}{38}\approx 2.37 . \] Takeaway. An expected value need not be a possible value — you can't win 2.37 games; it's the long-run average per 5-game session. For "expected number of successes in \(n\) tries", the answer is always \(np\), full stop.
Apply it — a risky project decision A project succeeds with probability 0.3, paying $500k; otherwise it loses $100k. Compute the expected profit and the standard deviation. Should a team that can only afford one attempt take it?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Expectation: \(E[X]=0.3(500)+0.7(-100)=150-70=\$80\text{k}\).
2 — Variance shortcut: \(E[X^2]=0.3(500^2)+0.7(100^2)=75\,000+7\,000=82\,000\); \(\mathrm{Var}=82\,000-80^2=75\,600\) ⇒ SD \(\approx\$275\text{k}\).
3 — Interpret: positive expectation but the SD dwarfs the mean — a single attempt loses money 70 % of the time. Expected value is a long-run average; for one-shot decisions you must also look at the distribution (here: can you survive the −$100k branch?).

6. Key distributions

Bernoulli and binomial

Bernoulli(\(p\)): one trial, \(X\in\{0,1\}\), \(E[X]=p\), \(\mathrm{Var}=p(1-p)\).
Binomial(\(n,p\)): number of successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials. \[ P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad E[X]=np,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(X)=np(1-p). \] Shape: symmetric when \(p=0.5\); skewed otherwise; approaches a normal curve as \(n\) grows (rule of thumb \(np\ge10\) and \(n(1-p)\ge10\)).

Every binomial question is solved the same way: (1) check the setup is \(n\) independent yes/no trials with the same success probability \(p\); (2) write down \(n\), \(p\), and which counts \(k\) the question asks about; (3) apply the PMF, summing over \(k\) or using a complement if needed. Watch each example follow those three steps:

Worked examples
  • Best-of-5 series. A team wins each game with \(p=0.75\); games independent. P(they win the series, i.e. at least 3 of 5)? Model: Binomial(\(n=5, p=0.75\)). The full PMF: \(P(0)=0.001,\ P(1)=0.015,\ P(2)=0.088,\ P(3)=0.264,\ P(4)=0.395,\ P(5)=0.237\). "At least 3" sums the last three: \(0.264+0.395+0.237=0.896\).
  • No red flowers. Each of 5 crosses gives a red flower with \(p=0.25\). P(zero reds)? That's \(k=0\): \(\binom50(0.25)^0(0.75)^5=0.75^5=0.237\) — with \(k=0\) or \(k=n\) the binomial coefficient is 1 and only the power survives. (Mock-exam Question 16.)
  • Win all 5 roulette games betting red (\(p=18/38\)): \(k=n=5\), so \(P=(18/38)^5\approx0.0238\). (Mock-exam Question 18.)
  • Exactly 2 egg sandwiches out of 3 customers, each choosing egg with \(p=0.7\): \(\binom32(0.7)^2(0.3)^1=3\times0.49\times0.3=0.441\). Don't forget the \(\binom32=3\): the two egg-buyers can be any 2 of the 3 customers. (Mock-exam Question 28.)
  • Exactly 2 boys of 3 children, \(p=0.51\) per child: \(\binom32(0.51)^2(0.49)^1=3\times0.2601\times0.49\approx0.382\approx0.38\). (Mock-exam Question 35.)
  • Exactly 4 of 6 patients recover. Careful: the problem says 75 % die, so the success ("recover") probability is \(p=0.25\). \(\binom64(0.25)^4(0.75)^2=15\times0.003906\times0.5625\approx0.0330\). Mislabeling which outcome is the "success" is the main trap here. (Mock-exam Question 33.)
  • Guessing 20 multiple-choice questions with 4 options (\(p=0.25\)): \(P(X=7)=\binom{20}{7}(0.25)^7(0.75)^{13}\approx0.112\); \(P(X\le7)\approx0.898\) (sum the PMF from 0 to 7 — or use software, see the cheat sheet).
  • Exactly 50 heads in 100 fair tosses: \(\binom{100}{50}(\tfrac12)^{100}\approx0.0796\) — note how unlikely the single most likely outcome is; "about half" is probable, "exactly half" is not.
Example — "which is most likely?" Question. Which event is most likely? (a) at least one 6 when rolling 6 dice; (b) at least two 6s with 12 dice; (c) at least three 6s with 18 dice. (Mock-exam Question 21 — all three "feel" identical since each expects its threshold on average.)
Step 1 — Compute each with the complement.
(a) \(P=1-P(\text{no 6 in 6})=1-(5/6)^6\approx0.665\).
(b) \(P=1-P(0\text{ or }1\text{ six in }12)=1-(5/6)^{12}-\binom{12}{1}(1/6)(5/6)^{11}\approx0.619\).
(c) same pattern over \(k=0,1,2\) with \(n=18\): \(\approx0.597\).
Step 2 — Compare. (a) > (b) > (c): rolling 6 dice for at least one 6 is the most likely.
Why they differ. Each case asks for "at least the mean" (means 1, 2, 3). As \(n\) grows the distribution concentrates tightly around its mean, and since "at least the mean" excludes nearly half the central bulk, its probability drifts down toward \(1/2\). Same expected count ≠ same probability.
Apply it — drone passes over a damaged field Each drone pass detects existing hail damage with probability 0.8, passes independent. With 3 passes: (a) P(detected at least once), (b) P(detected in exactly 2 passes), (c) expected number of detecting passes.
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Model: detections \(\sim\) Binomial\((n=3,p=0.8)\).
2 — (a) complement: \(1-(0.2)^3=0.992\).
3 — (b) binomial PMF: \(\binom32(0.8)^2(0.2)=3\times0.64\times0.2=0.384\).
4 — (c) mean: \(np=2.4\).
Rule check: the three answers used three different tools (complement, PMF, expectation) on one model — recognising the model first is what makes the rest mechanical.

Geometric and Poisson

Geometric(\(p\)) — number of trials until the first success: \(P(X=k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p\), \(E[X]=1/p\). Memoryless.
Poisson(\(\lambda\)) — count of events in a fixed interval when events arrive independently at rate \(\lambda\): \[ P(X=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!},\qquad E[X]=\mathrm{Var}(X)=\lambda . \] Approximates Binomial(\(n,p\)) when \(n\) large, \(p\) small, \(\lambda=np\).
Apply it — flaky test & weekly errors (a) A flaky CI failure reproduces on any run with probability 0.1. Expected runs to reproduce it, and P(more than 20 runs needed)? (b) A service throws errors at 0.5/day. P(at least one error next week)?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — (a) Geometric(0.1): \(E=1/p=10\) runs; \(P(X>20)=(0.9)^{20}\approx0.122\) — a 12 % chance the bug hides past 20 runs even though the mean is 10; geometric tails are long.
2 — (b) Poisson over a window: weekly count \(\sim\) Poisson\((\lambda=0.5\times7=3.5)\); \(P(\ge1)=1-e^{-3.5}\approx0.970\).
Rule check: geometric answers "how long until the first success", Poisson answers "how many events in a window" — pick by the question's shape, not by habit.

Uniform and exponential (continuous)

Uniform(\(a,b\)): \(f(x)=\dfrac1{b-a}\) on \([a,b]\); \(P(c\le X\le d)=\dfrac{d-c}{b-a}\); \(E=\dfrac{a+b}2\); \(\mathrm{Var}=\dfrac{(b-a)^2}{12}\).
Exponential(\(\lambda\)): \(f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x},\ x\ge0\); \(F(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}\); \(E=1/\lambda\); memoryless. Models waiting times between Poisson events.
Example — reading off an exponential probability Question. A random variable has density \(f(x)=\frac18e^{-x/8}\) for \(x\ge0\). Find \(P(X\le6)\). (Mock-exam Question 25.)
Step 1 — Recognise the distribution. Match \(f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\): here \(\lambda=\frac18\), so \(X\) is Exponential with rate \(\frac18\) and mean \(1/\lambda=8\).
Step 2 — Use the CDF instead of integrating from scratch. For the exponential, \(F(x)=P(X\le x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}\) (memorise this one).
Step 3 — Plug in. \[ P(X\le6)=1-e^{-6/8}=1-e^{-0.75}=1-0.4724=0.5276 . \] Sanity check. 6 is a bit below the mean 8, and the exponential piles most of its mass early, so "a bit over half" is plausible.
Example — uniform lifetime Question. An insect's lifetime is uniformly distributed between 0 and 16 days. What is P(it dies between day 5 and day 6)?
Step 1 — Uniform means flat density. \(f(x)=\frac1{16}\) everywhere on [0, 16]: every equal-length slice of the interval is equally likely.
Step 2 — Probability = length ratio. \[ P(5\le X\le6)=\frac{6-5}{16-0}=\frac1{16}\approx0.0625 . \] Related trap (mock-exam Question 40): P(it dies at exactly day 5) \(=0\) — a continuous variable never hits an exact point; only intervals carry probability.
Apply it — battery lifetimes and bus stops (a) A battery\'s lifetime is exponential with mean 5 years. P(it lasts beyond 8 years)? Given it has already survived 3 years, P(it reaches 8)? (b) Buses arrive every 15 minutes and you arrive at a uniformly random time. Expected wait and P(wait < 5 min)?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — (a) exponential survival: \(\lambda=1/5\); \(P(X>8)=e^{-8/5}=e^{-1.6}\approx0.202\).
2 — Memorylessness: \(P(X>8\mid X>3)=P(X>5)=e^{-1}\approx0.368\) — the 3 years already served are irrelevant.
3 — (b) uniform wait on [0, 15]: \(E=7.5\) min; \(P(W<5)=5/15=1/3\).
Contrast: the uniform wait has a hard ceiling (15 min); the exponential has no ceiling and no memory — two very different "waiting" models.

Normal distribution, CLT, z-scores

\[ f(x)=\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}},\qquad X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2) \] Symmetric about \(\mu\); mean = median = mode; larger \(\sigma\) ⇒ flatter and wider. Total area 1.
Empirical rule: \(\mu\pm1\sigma\) ≈ 68 %, \(\mu\pm2\sigma\) ≈ 95 %, \(\mu\pm3\sigma\) ≈ 99.7 %. So mean→+1σ ≈ 34 %, +1σ→+2σ ≈ 13.5 %, beyond +2σ ≈ 2.5 %.
z-score: \(z=\dfrac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\) (how many SDs from the mean); \(x=\mu+z\sigma\). Look up \(\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)\) in a table or scipy.stats.norm.cdf.

Every normal-distribution question is the same three moves: (1) standardise, \(z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\) ("how many SDs from the mean?"); (2) turn the question into an area under the standard normal curve; (3) get that area from the empirical rule (for \(z=1,2,3\)) or a table/software (for anything else). Sketching the curve and shading the region you want prevents most sign errors.

Worked examples
  • Between the mean and +1σ. Test scores are \(N(18, 6^2)\). P(score between 18 and 24)? Standardise both ends: \(z_1=\frac{18-18}{6}=0\), \(z_2=\frac{24-18}{6}=1\). The area between the mean and +1σ is half of the 68 % band: ≈ 34 %. (Mock-exam Question 19 — no table needed, just the empirical rule.)
  • An upper tail. Cholesterol levels are \(N(150, 15^2)\); a patient measures 172. \(z=\frac{172-150}{15}=1.47\); the table gives \(\Phi(1.47)=0.929\), so 93 % of people are below this patient and 7 % above — that's what "93rd percentile" means.
  • Another tail, from software. Exam scores \(N(67, 13.7^2)\): \(P(X\ge80)=1-\Phi\!\big(\frac{80-67}{13.7}\big)=1-\Phi(0.949)\approx0.171\).
  • IQ. \(N(100, 15^2)\): \(P(X>120)=1-\Phi(1.33)\approx0.091\) — about 9 % score above 120.
  • Spotting an outlier. A phone screen measures 9.5 cm in a process with \(\mu=9.99939\), \(\sigma=0.04878\). \(z=\frac{9.5-9.99939}{0.04878}\approx-10.2\). Ten SDs below the mean is essentially impossible for a normal process — this unit signals a process fault, not random variation. (z-scores as anomaly detectors.)
  • A three-claim question. Heights of 10-year-olds are \(N(55, 6^2)\) inches; which is true? (i) "more kids below 55 than above" — false: the normal is symmetric about its mean, so the halves are equal. (ii) "95 % between 37 and 73" — false: \(55\pm12=[43,67]\) is the 95 % (±2σ) band; 37–73 is ±3σ ≈ 99.7 %. (iii) "a 65-inch kid is more unusual than a 45-inch kid" — false: \(z=+1.67\) vs \(z=-1.67\), equally unusual by symmetry. Answer: none of these. (Mock-exam Question 36 — convert every claim to z-scores before judging it.)
Central limit theorem For i.i.d. \(X_i\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the sample mean \(\bar X_n\) is approximately \(N\!\left(\mu,\ \sigma^2/n\right)\) for large \(n\), whatever the population's shape. The standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) shrinks with \(\sqrt n\). This is why sample means (e.g. cholesterol averages of 50 people) pile up symmetrically around the population mean, and why the normal distribution is everywhere in statistics.
Apply it — crop yields, single plot vs field average Plot yields are \(N(3.2,\,0.5^2)\) t/ha. (a) P(a single plot yields under 2.5)? (b) For a field of 25 independent plots, P(the field average is under 3.0)?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — (a) z-score: \(z=\dfrac{2.5-3.2}{0.5}=-1.4\); \(\Phi(-1.4)\approx0.081\) — about 8 % of plots.
2 — (b) CLT for the mean: \(\bar X\sim N\big(3.2,\,(0.5/\sqrt{25})^2\big)=N(3.2,\,0.1^2)\).
3 — z-score again: \(z=\dfrac{3.0-3.2}{0.1}=-2\); \(\Phi(-2)\approx0.023\).
Interpret: a single low plot is common (8 %), a low field average is rare (2.3 %) — averaging shrinks the spread by \(\sqrt n\). Mixing up the SD of one observation with the SE of a mean is the most common normal-distribution error in interviews.

Show the step-by-step solution

8. Classic traps (where marks are lost)

  1. Disjoint ≠ independent. Disjoint: \(P(A\cap B)=0\). Independent: \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\).
  2. Base-rate neglect. A 99 %-accurate test for a 0.1 % disease still gives mostly false positives. Always multiply by the prior.
  3. Confusing \(P(A\mid B)\) with \(P(B\mid A)\) (the "prosecutor's fallacy"). Bayes is the bridge.
  4. Gambler's fallacy. Independent trials have no memory.
  5. Continuous variables: \(P(X=x)=0\); PDF values are densities and can exceed 1.
  6. Sampling without replacement changes probabilities (19/59, not 1/3; 3/6 · 2/5).
  7. "At least one" → compute \(1-P(\text{none})\).
  8. Order matters or not? Permutations vs combinations; for ordered draws the sample space is ordered pairs (boy–girl paradox).
  9. Empirical rule limits: 68/95/99.7 are for ±1/±2/±3σ, not other multiples.
  10. Monty Hall: the host's knowledge makes the door opening informative. Switch.
  11. Expected value sign and units: subtract the cost; keep track of whose perspective (insurer vs customer).
  12. \(P(A)\) must be in \([0,1]\) and \(P(A\cup B)\le1\) — a quick sanity check on any answer.

Part II — Advanced probability

Everything below goes beyond the mock exam. Part I is what a data-science interviewer expects you to know cold; Part II is what separates a good answer from a great one — and what quant-style questions (like the "coin flips → balls → bins" problem) actually test. Each section has the same ICAP ladder: read, explore, self-check, build your own, then spar with the tutor.

How to attack a multi-step probability problem
  1. Name the random variables and the stages of the process (what is random first, what depends on it).
  2. Condition on the first stage: write \(P(\text{event})=\sum_k P(\text{event}\mid K=k)\,P(K=k)\) or \(E[X]=E\big[E[X\mid K]\big]\).
  3. For "none / all / at least one" events use complement + inclusion–exclusion.
  4. For expectations of counts use indicator variables and linearity — never enumerate.
  5. Sum the series (geometric series are the usual ones), then sanity-check with small cases (k = 1, 2) and with bounds (is it in [0,1]? does it move the right way when a parameter changes?).

A1. Inclusion–exclusion as a tool

For events \(A_1,\dots,A_n\): \[ P\Big(\bigcup_{i} A_i\Big)=\sum_i P(A_i)-\sum_{i
Example — balls into bins (the core of the quant question) Question. \(k\) balls are dropped independently, each landing in one of \(m\) bins uniformly at random. What is the probability that no bin is empty?
Step 1 — Define the "bad" events. Let \(A_i\) = "bin \(i\) ends up empty". We want \(P(\text{no bin empty})=1-P(A_1\cup\dots\cup A_m)\).
Step 2 — Probabilities of the pieces. Bin \(i\) is empty when all \(k\) balls avoid it — each ball does so with probability \(1-\frac1m\), independently: \(P(A_i)=(1-\frac1m)^k\). Two specified bins both empty: all balls avoid both, \(P(A_i\cap A_j)=(1-\frac2m)^k\). And so on — avoiding \(j\) specified bins has probability \((1-\frac jm)^k\).
Step 3 — Inclusion–exclusion over the \(\binom mj\) ways to pick which bins are empty: \[ P(\text{no bin empty})=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^j\binom{m}{j}\Big(1-\frac{j}{m}\Big)^{k} . \] Step 4 — Specialise to \(m=3\) (the interview case): \(P=1-3\big(\tfrac23\big)^k+3\big(\tfrac13\big)^k\) — the \(j=3\) term vanishes since all three bins can't be empty.
Step 5 — Sanity-check on tiny cases. \(k=3\): formula gives \(1-\tfrac89+\tfrac19=\tfrac29\); direct count: the 3 balls must occupy all 3 bins, \(3!=6\) favourable placements out of \(3^3=27\), \(\tfrac6{27}=\tfrac29\) ✓. \(k=4\): \(1-\tfrac{48}{81}+\tfrac3{81}=\tfrac{36}{81}=\tfrac49\). Also check limits: \(k<3\Rightarrow P=0\) (too few balls) and \(P\to1\) as \(k\to\infty\) — the formula obeys both.
Example — derangements (Secret Santa) Question. \(n\) people each draw a name from a hat (a random one-to-one assignment). What is the probability nobody draws their own name?
Step 1 — Set up for inclusion–exclusion. Let \(A_i\) = "person \(i\) draws themselves". We want \(1-P(\cup A_i)\). The events overlap heavily, so the alternating sum is required; the algebra collapses to a famous series: \[ P(\text{no fixed point})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^j}{j!}=1-1+\frac1{2!}-\frac1{3!}+\cdots \] Step 2 — Notice how fast it converges. That's the Taylor series of \(e^{-1}\approx0.368\). Already at \(n=4\) the exact value is \(0.375\) — for any realistic group size, "nobody gets themselves" happens about 37 % of the time, essentially independent of \(n\). A great sanity anchor to memorise.
Classic follow-up. "Expected number of people who draw themselves?" Don't touch inclusion–exclusion — use one indicator per person: each has probability \(\frac1n\) of self-drawing, so \(E=n\times\frac1n=1\), for every \(n\) (see A3).
Apply it — reviewers and bins (a) Five reviewers are randomly assigned one paper each (their own papers are among the five). P(nobody reviews their own paper)? (b) Six balls are dropped uniformly into 3 bins. P(no bin empty)?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — (a) derangement via inclusion–exclusion: \(\sum_{j=0}^{5}\frac{(-1)^j}{j!}=1-1+\tfrac12-\tfrac16+\tfrac1{24}-\tfrac1{120}=\tfrac{44}{120}\approx0.367\) — already near \(e^{-1}\).
2 — (b) the bins formula: \(1-3\big(\tfrac23\big)^6+3\big(\tfrac13\big)^6=1-\tfrac{192}{729}+\tfrac{3}{729}=\tfrac{540}{729}\approx0.741\).
Rule check: in both, the events being excluded ("reviewer \(i\) gets own paper", "bin \(i\) empty") overlap, so the alternating sum corrects the double counting that a naive \(\sum P(A_i)\) commits.

A2. Conditioning on a random first stage

Law of total probability (countable partition) \(\;P(E)=\sum_{k} P(E\mid K=k)\,P(K=k)\).
Law of total expectation (tower rule) \(\;E[X]=E\big[E[X\mid K]\big]\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[\mathrm{Var}(X\mid K)]+\mathrm{Var}(E[X\mid K])\).
Wald's identity — sum of a random number \(N\) of i.i.d. terms: \(E[X_1+\dots+X_N]=E[N]\,E[X]\) (when \(N\) does not peek at the future).
Worked — the quant problem, fully Flip a fair coin until the first head; first head on flip \(K\) gives \(K\) balls, placed uniformly into 3 bins. \(P(\text{no bin empty})\)?
Stage 1: \(K\sim\text{Geometric}(\tfrac12)\): \(P(K=k)=2^{-k}\), \(k\ge1\).
Stage 2 (A1): \(P(\text{none empty}\mid K=k)=1-3(2/3)^k+3(1/3)^k\).
Combine: \[ \sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k}\Big[1-3\big(\tfrac23\big)^k+3\big(\tfrac13\big)^k\Big] =\sum_k\big(\tfrac12\big)^k-3\sum_k\big(\tfrac13\big)^k+3\sum_k\big(\tfrac16\big)^k = 1-3\cdot\tfrac12+3\cdot\tfrac15=\tfrac1{10}. \] (Geometric series: \(\sum_{k\ge1}r^k=\frac{r}{1-r}\).) Sanity: \(k\le2\) contributes 0, \(k=3\) contributes \(\tfrac18\cdot\tfrac29\approx0.028\), and the tail adds up to 0.1 — plausible.
Worked — a random number of summands (Wald) Question. A store receives a random number of customers per day, \(N\sim\text{Poisson}(20)\); each customer independently spends a random amount averaging $35. What is the expected daily revenue?
The subtlety. Revenue is \(X_1+\dots+X_N\) — a sum with a random number of terms, so you can't just "add 20 things". Wald's identity handles exactly this: \[ E[X_1+\dots+X_N]=E[N]\cdot E[X]=20\times35=\$700 . \] Intuition: on average there are 20 terms, each worth $35 on average, and (crucially) how many customers show up doesn't depend on how much each one spends.
Variance is not so simple. Two sources of randomness — spending varies and headcount varies — so the tower rule adds them: \(\mathrm{Var}=E[N]\,\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(N)\,E[X]^2\). Forgetting the second term (busy vs quiet days) badly understates daily swings.
Apply it — die, then coins Roll a fair die; if it shows \(k\), flip \(k\) fair coins. Probability of getting no heads at all?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Condition on the first stage: \(P(\text{no heads}\mid K=k)=(1/2)^k\).
2 — Law of total probability: \(P=\sum_{k=1}^{6}\tfrac16\big(\tfrac12\big)^k=\tfrac16\Big(1-\tfrac1{64}\Big)=\tfrac16\cdot\tfrac{63}{64}=\tfrac{21}{128}\approx0.164\).
3 — Bonus (tower rule): expected number of heads \(=E[K]\cdot\tfrac12=3.5\times0.5=1.75\).
Sanity: the answer must sit between \((1/2)^6\approx0.016\) and \((1/2)^1=0.5\) — it does, closer to the small-\(k\) cases because they dominate the "no heads" event.

A3. Expectation tricks: indicators, first-step analysis, memorylessness

Indicators. For a count \(X=\sum_i \mathbf 1_{A_i}\): \(E[X]=\sum_i P(A_i)\) — works even when the \(A_i\) are dependent.
Tail-sum formula. For non-negative integer \(X\): \(E[X]=\sum_{k\ge1}P(X\ge k)\).
First-step analysis. Condition on the first trial and write an equation for the unknown expectation.
Memorylessness. Geometric and exponential: \(P(X>s+t\mid X>s)=P(X>t)\). The past does not shorten the wait.
Worked examples
  • Coupon collector. How many die rolls, on average, until every face 1–6 has appeared at least once? Break the wait into phases by how many distinct faces you've seen. With \(i\) faces already seen, each roll shows a new face with probability \(\frac{6-i}{6}\), so that phase lasts Geometric time with mean \(\frac{6}{6-i}\) rolls. Add the phases (linearity): \(E=\frac66+\frac65+\frac64+\frac63+\frac62+\frac61=6\big(1+\frac12+\dots+\frac16\big)=6H_6\approx14.7\) rolls. (Mock-exam-adjacent classic; general answer \(nH_n\approx n\ln n\).) Note the phases get slower — the last face alone costs 6 rolls on average.
  • Expected maximum of two dice. Roll two dice; what is \(E[\max]\)? The tail-sum formula turns this into survival probabilities: \(E[\max]=\sum_{k=1}^{6}P(\max\ge k)\). And \(P(\max\ge k)=1-P(\text{both}
  • Expected flips until HH vs HT. Flip a fair coin until you see the pattern HH (two heads in a row). Expected flips? Let \(E\) be the answer, starting from scratch. Condition on how the attempt unfolds: flip once (cost 1). If it's T (prob ½) you're back to scratch: expect \(E\) more. If it's H, flip again (cost 1): if H, done; if T, back to scratch. As an equation: \(E=1+\frac12E+\frac12\big(1+\frac12\cdot0+\frac12E\big)\). Solve: \(E=\frac32+\frac34E\Rightarrow E=6\). The same method for the pattern HT gives 4. Why easier for HT? When an HH attempt fails (H then T), you lose everything; when an HT attempt "fails" (H then H), the last H still counts as a fresh start toward HT. Progress is never fully lost, so HT arrives sooner.
  • Fixed points of a random permutation. \(n\) hats returned at random; expected number of people with their own hat? One indicator per person, each with \(P=\frac1n\): \(E=n\cdot\frac1n=1\) — regardless of \(n\), and despite the indicators being dependent (linearity doesn't care).
  • Expected number of distinct birthdays among 30 people. One indicator per day: day \(d\) is hit unless all 30 people miss it, so \(P(\text{hit})=1-(\frac{364}{365})^{30}\). Sum over days: \(E=365\big[1-(364/365)^{30}\big]\approx28.8\) — just below 30 because a few birthdays collide.
Apply it — counting with indicators (a) 20 people: expected number of pairs sharing a birthday? (b) 10 balls thrown uniformly into 5 bins: expected number of empty bins?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — (a) one indicator per pair: \(E=\binom{20}{2}\cdot\dfrac1{365}=\dfrac{190}{365}\approx0.52\) — about half a pair on average, matching the ~50 % chance of at least one match at \(n=23\).
2 — (b) one indicator per bin: \(P(\text{bin } i \text{ empty})=(4/5)^{10}\approx0.107\), so \(E=5\times0.107\approx0.54\).
Rule check: the pair events and the bin events are dependent, and linearity did not care. Whenever a question says "expected number of …", reach for indicators before anything else.

Example — portfolio / ensemble variance Question. You average \(n\) predictors (or asset returns), each with variance \(\sigma^2\), every pair correlated at \(\rho\). How much does averaging reduce the variance?

1 — Bilinearity: \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,S)=\mathrm{Cov}(X,X)+\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+0=\tfrac{35}{12}\approx2.92\).
2 — Variance of the sum (independence): \(\mathrm{Var}(S)=2\times\tfrac{35}{12}\).
3 — Correlation: \(\rho=\dfrac{\mathrm{Var}(X)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}\sqrt{2\mathrm{Var}(X)}}=\dfrac1{\sqrt2}\approx0.707\).
Interpret: a total is correlated \(1/\sqrt2\) with each of its two equal parts — the same arithmetic explains why a portfolio co-moves with each holding, and why a model ensemble correlates with its members.

A5. Transformations and order statistics

CDF method. For \(Y=g(X)\) compute \(F_Y(y)=P(g(X)\le y)\) then differentiate. For monotone \(g\): \(f_Y(y)=f_X(g^{-1}(y))\,\big|\tfrac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)\big|\).
Inverse-CDF sampling. If \(U\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\) then \(F^{-1}(U)\sim F\); e.g. \(-\ln(U)/\lambda\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)\).
Max / min of i.i.d. \(P(\max\le x)=F(x)^n\), \(P(\min> x)=(1-F(x))^n\).
Worked examples
  • Max and min of uniforms. Take \(n\) independent Uniform(0,1) draws. Where do the extremes sit on average? \(E[\max]=\frac{n}{n+1}\) and \(E[\min]=\frac1{n+1}\) — with \(n\) points the interval splits into \(n+1\) roughly equal gaps, so the largest lands about one gap short of 1. (Derivation of the max: \(P(\max\le x)=x^n\) since every draw must be \(\le x\); differentiate for the density, or integrate the survival function.) More generally the \(k\)-th smallest is Beta\((k,\,n+1-k)\).
  • Racing exponentials. Servers A and B answer in independent Exp(\(\lambda_A\)), Exp(\(\lambda_B\)) times. Two facts solve every "who answers first" question: the first response arrives in \(\min\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda_A+\lambda_B)\) time (rates add — someone answers faster than either alone), and \(P(B\text{ first})=\frac{\lambda_B}{\lambda_A+\lambda_B}\) (each racer wins in proportion to its rate). E.g. \(\lambda_A=1,\lambda_B=3\) per minute: B first with probability \(\frac34\), first answer in mean \(\frac14\) min. This is the "competing risks" pattern — machine failures, first ticket to arrive, etc.
  • Squares of normals. If \(Z\sim N(0,1)\) then \(Z^2\sim\chi^2_1\), and a sum of \(n\) independent squared standard normals is \(\chi^2_n\) — this is why chi-square distributions appear whenever you sum squared standardised errors (goodness-of-fit tests, variance estimates).
Apply it — sensor fleet Three independent sensors each have Exp(1/10 yr) lifetimes. (a) Distribution and mean of the time to the first failure? (b) P(all three survive 5 years)?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — (a) min of exponentials: rates add: first failure \(\sim\text{Exp}(3/10)\), mean \(10/3\approx3.3\) years — three sensors fail three times as fast as one.
2 — (b) independence product: \(P=\big(e^{-5/10}\big)^3=e^{-1.5}\approx0.223\) — the same number as \(P(\text{Exp}(3/10)>5)\), confirming (a).
3 — Bonus: P(sensor 2 is the first to fail) \(=\dfrac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3}=\dfrac13\) by symmetry.

A6. Poisson process

Events arriving "at random" at rate \(\lambda\): counts in any window of length \(t\) are Poisson\((\lambda t)\); gaps between events are i.i.d. Exp\((\lambda)\); counts in disjoint windows are independent.
Thinning: keeping each event independently with probability \(p\) gives a Poisson process of rate \(p\lambda\). Superposition: merging independent processes adds the rates.
Poisson approximation: Binomial\((n,p)\approx\) Poisson\((np)\) for large \(n\), small \(p\) — rare-event counts (defects, fraud, hail strikes per field).
Worked examples
  • Tickets in a window. Support tickets arrive at 3/hour. P(no ticket in the next 20 minutes)? First convert the rate to the window asked about: 20 min = ⅓ hour, so the count in that window is Poisson(\(3\times\frac13=1\)) and \(P(0)=e^{-1}\approx0.368\). P(at least 2 in an hour)? Count ∼ Poisson(3); complement over 0 and 1: \(1-e^{-3}(1+3)\approx0.801\).
  • Thinning. 10 % of those tickets are urgent (independently). Randomly keeping a fraction of a Poisson process leaves a Poisson process at the reduced rate: urgent tickets arrive at \(0.1\times3=0.3\)/hour, so the gap to the next urgent ticket is Exp(0.3), mean \(1/0.3\approx3.3\) hours — and by memorylessness that stays true no matter how long you've already been waiting.
  • Poisson as a rare-event approximation. 1 000 fields, each independently hail-damaged with probability 0.002. The damaged count is Binomial(1000, 0.002), but with \(n\) huge and \(p\) tiny it's ≈ Poisson(\(np=2\)): \(P(0)=e^{-2}\approx0.135\), \(P(\ge3)=1-e^{-2}(1+2+2)\approx0.323\). Much easier than binomial coefficients with \(n=1000\).
Apply it — request spikes and thinning A web service receives 120 requests/min. (a) P(a given second gets more than 2 requests)? (b) 5 % of requests are API calls — what process do the API calls follow, and what is the expected gap between them?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — (a) restate the rate for the window: 120/min = 2/sec, so the per-second count is Poisson(2).
2 — Complement over 0, 1, 2: \(P(\ge3)=1-e^{-2}(1+2+2)=1-5e^{-2}\approx0.323\).
3 — (b) thinning: independent 5 % selection of a Poisson process is again Poisson, rate \(0.05\times120=6\)/min; gaps are Exp(6/min), expected gap 10 s.
Rule check: converting the rate to the window you are asked about (step 1) is where most mistakes happen.

A7. Markov chains

A sequence \(X_0,X_1,\dots\) where the next state depends only on the current one: \(P(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i)=P_{ij}\). Rows of the transition matrix \(P\) sum to 1. \(n\)-step probabilities are \(P^n\). A stationary distribution \(\pi\) satisfies \(\pi P=\pi\) and, for an irreducible aperiodic chain, is the long-run fraction of time in each state.
Absorption / first-step analysis: to find \(h_i=P(\text{absorbed in } A\mid X_0=i)\) solve \(h_i=\sum_j P_{ij}h_j\) with \(h=1\) on \(A\) and \(0\) on the other absorbing set.
Worked examples
  • Gambler's ruin. You start with \$\(i\) and bet \$1 on a fair coin each round, stopping when you hit \$0 (ruin) or \$\(N\) (target). P(you reach the target)? States 0…\(N\); 0 and \(N\) are absorbing. For the fair game the answer is beautifully simple: \(P(\text{reach }N)=\frac iN\) — e.g. start \$3, target \$10 ⇒ 30 %. (Why it's plausible: a fair game keeps your expected fortune constant at \(i\); the only way \(E=\!i\) at the end is \(N\cdot P+0\cdot(1-P)=i\), forcing \(P=i/N\).) For an unfair game with win probability \(p\ne\frac12\), set \(r=\frac{1-p}{p}\): \(P=\frac{1-r^i}{1-r^N}\) — even a small house edge crushes you for large \(N\). Expected number of rounds (fair): \(i(N-i)\), e.g. \(3\times7=21\) rounds.
  • Weather chain / steady state. Sunny today → sunny tomorrow with 0.8; rainy → sunny with 0.4. What fraction of days is sunny in the long run? The stationary distribution balances the flows between states: (chance of leaving S) × \(\pi_S\) = (chance of entering S) × \(\pi_R\), i.e. \(0.2\,\pi_S=0.4\,\pi_R\), so \(\pi_S=2\pi_R\); with \(\pi_S+\pi_R=1\) this gives \(\pi=(\frac23,\frac13)\) — two sunny days out of three, regardless of today's weather.
  • The same machinery everywhere. PageRank is the stationary distribution of a random web surfer; hidden Markov models put noisy observations on top of a Markov chain; MCMC builds a chain whose stationary distribution is the one you want to sample.
Apply it — churn steady state Each month 10 % of active users churn; each month 2 % of churned users reactivate. In the long run, what fraction of users is active?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — Model as a 2-state Markov chain with \(P(\text{A}\to\text{C})=0.1\), \(P(\text{C}\to\text{A})=0.02\).
2 — Stationarity balances the flows: \(\pi_A\times0.1=\pi_C\times0.02\Rightarrow\pi_C=5\pi_A\).
3 — Normalise: \(\pi_A=\dfrac16\approx16.7\,\%\) active, \(83.3\,\%\) churned.
Interpret: the long-run split depends only on the ratio of the two rates — halving churn or doubling reactivation moves the steady state identically. This balance-the-flows shortcut solves any 2-state chain instantly.

A8. Inequalities, LLN, CLT — how sure can you be?

Markov: \(X\ge0\Rightarrow P(X\ge a)\le E[X]/a\).   Chebyshev: \(P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le1/k^2\).   Chernoff/Hoeffding (bounded i.i.d.): \(P(|\bar X-\mu|\ge t)\le2e^{-2nt^2/(b-a)^2}\).
Law of large numbers: \(\bar X_n\to\mu\). CLT: \(\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma\to N(0,1)\); the error of a sample mean shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\) — to halve it you need 4× the data.
Delta method: \(g(\bar X_n)\approx N\big(g(\mu),\,g'(\mu)^2\sigma^2/n\big)\).
Worked examples
  • Estimate a proportion to ±2 % with 95 % confidence: \(n=(1.96\cdot0.5/0.02)^2\approx2401\) (worst case \(p=0.5\)). For ±1 %: 9604.
  • A metric has mean 50, SD 10. Chebyshev: at most \(1/9\approx11\%\) of values lie outside \([20,80]\) whatever the distribution; if normal the true figure is 0.27 %.
  • Simulation accuracy: a Monte Carlo estimate of a probability \(p\) from \(n\) runs has SE \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) — 10 000 runs give about ±1 %.
Apply it — latency guarantees Response latency has mean 200 ms and SD 50 ms, distribution unknown. (a) Bound P(latency outside [50, 350] ms). (b) If latency were normal, what would that probability be? (c) How many samples to estimate the mean within ±10 ms at 95 % confidence?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — (a) Chebyshev (no distributional assumption): 150 ms \(=3\sigma\), so \(P\le1/3^2=1/9\approx11\,\%\).
2 — (b) normal: \(2\Phi(-3)\approx0.27\,\%\) — Chebyshev is loose but unconditional.
3 — (c) CLT sample size: \(n=\Big(\dfrac{1.96\times50}{10}\Big)^2\approx96\).
Interpret: use Chebyshev when you must defend a claim with no assumptions (SLAs, adversarial reviews); use the normal numbers when the CLT or data justifies them.

A9. From probability to inference (what DS interviews actually ask)

Likelihood & MLE. \(L(\theta)=\prod_i f(x_i\mid\theta)\); maximise \(\log L\). Bernoulli: \(\hat p=k/n\). Normal: \(\hat\mu=\bar x\), \(\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\) (biased; divide by \(n-1\) for unbiased). Logistic regression = MLE of a Bernoulli model; least squares = MLE under Gaussian noise.
Confidence interval for a proportion: \(\hat p\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\); for a mean: \(\bar x\pm t_{n-1}\,s/\sqrt n\). Meaning: the procedure captures the truth 95 % of the time — not "95 % probability the truth is in this interval" (that is the Bayesian credible interval).
Hypothesis test & p-value. \(p=P(\text{data at least this extreme}\mid H_0)\). It is not \(P(H_0\mid\text{data})\) and not the probability the result is due to chance. Type I error \(\alpha\) (false alarm), Type II \(\beta\) (miss), power \(=1-\beta\).
A/B test sample size (two proportions, per group): \(n\approx\dfrac{\big(z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p\bar q}+z_{\beta}\sqrt{p_1q_1+p_2q_2}\big)^2}{(p_1-p_2)^2}\); rule of thumb for means: \(n\approx16\sigma^2/\delta^2\) at \(\alpha=0.05\), power 0.8.
Multiple comparisons. Testing 20 metrics at \(\alpha=0.05\) gives \(1-0.95^{20}=64\%\) chance of at least one false positive; Bonferroni uses \(\alpha/m\); Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false-discovery rate.
Probability inside ML models
  • Precision is Bayes' theorem. With recall (sensitivity) \(r\), false-positive rate \(f\) and prevalence \(\pi\): \(\text{precision}=\dfrac{r\pi}{r\pi+f(1-\pi)}\). Recall 90 %, FPR 5 %, prevalence 2 % ⇒ precision \(=0.018/(0.018+0.049)=27\%\). Class imbalance is base-rate neglect in disguise — the same arithmetic as the ELISA example.
  • ROC-AUC \(=P(\text{score of a random positive}>\text{score of a random negative})\). Precision–recall curves are the right view when positives are rare (hail-damaged fields, fraud).
  • Log-loss \(=-\frac1n\sum[y\log\hat p+(1-y)\log(1-\hat p)]\) is the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli model; calibration means \(\hat p=0.7\) is right about 70 % of the time (reliability diagram).
  • Entropy \(H=-\sum p\log p\), KL divergence \(D(P\|Q)=\sum p\log\frac pq\ge0\), cross-entropy \(=H(P)+D(P\|Q)\) — why "cross-entropy loss" is just MLE.
  • Naive Bayes assumes features independent given the class; Bayesian updating with a Beta prior: Beta\((a,b)\) + \(k\) successes in \(n\) trials ⇒ Beta\((a+k,\,b+n-k)\) — the basis of Bayesian A/B tests and Thompson sampling.
Apply it — sizing a conversion A/B test Baseline conversion is 5 %; you care about a lift to 5.5 %. (a) Sample size per arm at \(\alpha=0.05\), power 80 %? (b) A hail-damage classifier has recall 85 % and FPR 8 % at 5 % prevalence — precision?
Show the step-by-step solution
1 — (a) plug into the two-proportion formula with \(\bar p=0.0525\): \(n=\dfrac{\big(1.96\sqrt{2(0.0525)(0.9475)}+0.8416\sqrt{0.05(0.95)+0.055(0.945)}\big)^2}{0.005^2}\approx31\,000\) per arm.
2 — Interpret: detecting a 0.5-point lift on a 5 % base needs ~62k users total — small lifts on small bases are expensive; this is why teams test bigger changes or use variance-reduction (CUPED).
3 — (b) precision is Bayes: \(\dfrac{0.85\times0.05}{0.85\times0.05+0.08\times0.95}=\dfrac{0.0425}{0.1185}\approx36\,\%\).
Rule check: both are the same discipline — quantify the base rate before promising performance.

A10. Simulation and the bootstrap

When the algebra is ugly, simulate. Monte Carlo: draw many samples, count. Bootstrap: resample your data with replacement many times, recompute the statistic, and read the spread as its sampling distribution (percentile CI = 2.5th–97.5th percentile of the bootstrap estimates). Permutation test: shuffle labels to get the null distribution of a test statistic. Always fix a seed for reproducibility and report the Monte Carlo standard error. 
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

# The quant problem by simulation: flip until first head, put K balls in 3 bins
def trial():
    k = rng.geometric(0.5)                  # first head on flip k
    bins = rng.integers(0, 3, size=k)       # uniform bins
    return len(set(bins)) == 3
N = 200_000
p_hat = np.mean([trial() for _ in range(N)])
print(p_hat, "±", np.sqrt(p_hat*(1-p_hat)/N))   # ≈ 0.100 ± 0.0007

# Coupon collector, expected rolls to see all 6 faces
def rolls_to_collect(n=6):
    seen, t = set(), 0
    while len(seen) < n:
        seen.add(rng.integers(n)); t += 1
    return t
print(np.mean([rolls_to_collect() for _ in range(20_000)]))   # ≈ 14.7

# Bootstrap 95% CI for a mean
x = rng.exponential(8, size=60)
boots = [rng.choice(x, size=len(x), replace=True).mean() for _ in range(5000)]
print(np.percentile(boots, [2.5, 97.5]))

# Gambler's ruin, fair, start 3, target 10
def ruin(i=3, N=10):
    while 0 < i < N: i += rng.choice([-1, 1])
    return i == N
print(np.mean([ruin() for _ in range(20_000)]))   # ≈ 0.30

# Two-proportion A/B sample size per group
from scipy.stats import norm
def n_per_group(p1, p2, alpha=0.05, power=0.8):
    za, zb = norm.ppf(1-alpha/2), norm.ppf(power)
    pbar = (p1+p2)/2
    return ((za*np.sqrt(2*pbar*(1-pbar)) + zb*np.sqrt(p1*(1-p1)+p2*(1-p2)))**2) / (p1-p2)**2
print(round(n_per_group(0.10, 0.12)))   # ≈ 3800 per group

A11. Quant-style practice set (no answers here — use the tutor)

Attempt each within 5 minutes on paper, then paste your reasoning into the constructive box below and ask the tutor to critique it. Each question has a hint naming the tool to reach for — try without it first.
  1. Flip a fair coin until the first head; receive \(K\) balls; place them uniformly into 2 bins. Probability both bins are non-empty?
    HintSame two-stage structure as the worked A2 problem, but with \(m=2\) bins. Condition on \(K=k\) (\(P(K=k)=2^{-k}\)). Given \(k\ge1\) balls, "bin 1 empty" and "bin 2 empty" are disjoint, so P(some bin empty | k) \(=2\cdot(1/2)^k\) and P(both hit | k) \(=1-2^{1-k}\). Then sum the geometric series over \(k\).
  2. Roll a die until a 6 appears. Expected sum of all rolls including the 6?
    HintWald's identity: number of rolls is Geometric(1/6) with mean 6; but the non-6 rolls average \((1+2+3+4+5)/5=3\), not 3.5. Split: E[sum] = E[sum of non-6 rolls] + 6, with a Geometric-minus-one count of non-6 rolls.
  3. Two people each pick a uniformly random integer from 1–100. Expected value of the larger number? (Use the tail-sum formula.)
    HintMirror the "expected maximum of two dice" example in A3 with 100 in place of 6: \(E[\max]=\sum_{k=1}^{100}\big[1-\big(\frac{k-1}{100}\big)^2\big]\). Answer lands near \(\frac{2}{3}\times100\).
  4. A bag has 3 red and 2 blue balls; draw without replacement until the first red. Expected number of draws?
    HintOnly 3 possible paths: red on draw 1; blue then red; blue, blue, then red — after both blues are gone only reds remain, so the walk can't last past 3 draws. Compute each path's probability with the chain rule (updating the bag), then take the weighted average of 1, 2, 3.
  5. You have a biased coin with unknown \(p\). Design a procedure that produces a fair bit. Expected number of flips?
    HintVon Neumann's trick: flip twice; HT ↦ 0, TH ↦ 1 (equally likely by symmetry!), and on HH/TT discard and repeat. Each round of 2 flips succeeds with probability \(2p(1-p)\) — the round count is geometric.
  6. Emails arrive as a Poisson process at 12/hour; each is spam with probability 0.7 independently. Probability that in 10 minutes you get at least one non-spam email?
    HintThin the process: non-spam mail is Poisson at \(0.3\times12=3.6\)/hour. Convert the rate to a 10-minute window, then use \(1-P(0)\).
  7. A random walk starts at 0 and moves ±1 with equal probability. Probability it hits +3 before −2?
    HintShift so the boundaries are 0 and \(N\): starting point \(i=2\), target \(N=5\). Gambler's ruin with a fair coin gives \(i/N\).
  8. An A/B test with 5 000 users per arm measures conversion 4.0 % vs 4.6 %. Is the difference significant at 5 %? What sample size would detect a 0.6-point lift with 80 % power?
    HintTwo-proportion z-test: SE \(=\sqrt{\bar p\bar q(\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2})}\) with pooled \(\bar p=0.043\); compare \(z=0.006/\text{SE}\) to 1.96. For the sample size, plug into the A9 formula (or the Python helper in A10).
  9. A classifier has AUC 0.95 but precision 15 % at the chosen threshold on a 1 % prevalence problem. Explain how both can be true and what you would change.
    HintAUC is rank-based and prevalence-blind; precision is Bayes with a 1 % prior, so even a small FPR floods the positives. Think: precision formula in A9, PR curve instead of ROC, threshold moved for the intended action.
  10. Estimate \(\pi\) by Monte Carlo and give the number of samples needed for two correct decimal places with 95 % confidence.
    HintDarts in the unit square: P(inside the quarter circle) \(=\pi/4\approx0.785\). Estimating \(\pi=4p\) to ±0.005 means estimating \(p\) to ±0.00125, so \(n=(1.96)^2\,p(1-p)/0.00125^2\approx4\times10^5\) samples — accuracy scales as \(1/\sqrt n\), so each extra decimal place costs 100× the samples.
